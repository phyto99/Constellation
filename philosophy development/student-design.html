Part I · Section 1
The Terminal — What the Student Sees

The terminal is the only moment the system speaks directly to the student. It appears before each session. It does not explain the game. It does not give instructions. It communicates trajectory.

The language is designed to create a specific feeling before the first move: that this session was chosen for a reason, and that reason is about the student's position in something larger than today's game.

Constraint: the terminal never says "you are in SPATIAL mode" or "you ranked 73rd percentile." It speaks about the student's relationship to cognitive territory, not about the system's internal variables. The system knows the variables. The student experiences the story.

Part I · Section 2
Zone Names — The Vocabulary of Trajectory

Zone names are the only labels the student ever sees for their position. They describe the relationship between the student and the current session, not the cognitive mode or difficulty level. The student does not need to know they are in QTY mode. They need to know what kind of moment this is.

The five names form a complete vocabulary of challenge relationships. A student who has experienced all five has experienced the full range of how the system positions sessions relative to their current ability.

ECHO
Trigger: high similarity to recent sessions, same mode, ZPD comfortable
The student is being asked to confirm something already learned. Reinforcement. The session should feel familiar but not easy — familiar in structure, demanding in execution.
"You have been here before. The question is whether you've made it yours."
PARALLEL
Trigger: same mode, ZPD at ceiling, parameters shifted
The cognitive domain is the same but the conditions have changed. The student must apply existing skill in a novel context. Tests transfer within a mode.
"Same territory, different weather. What you know still applies — but not directly."
SHIFTING
Trigger: mode change from last session, or INVERSION config
Something structurally different from the last session. The student enters without knowing exactly what changed. Diagnostic — the system is watching how they adapt to unfamiliar structure.
"The rules of the last session don't apply here. Find the new ones."
RADICAL
Trigger: compiler selects a mode with large deficit, first session in a long-stale mode
The system is deliberately placing the student outside their comfort zone. This should feel slightly destabilizing. High information gain expected.
"This session targets a gap the system has been watching. You may not perform well. That is useful."
REMATCH
Trigger: repeating a session type where student previously ranked below 0.40
The system is returning the student to something they did not do well. Not punishment — the signal from the previous attempt was incomplete. The system needs more data, and the student needs the chance to develop.
"The system remembers the last time you were here. So do you."
Part I · Section 3
Mystery vs Transparency — The Calibration Problem

Every disclosure decision is a tradeoff between two goods: transparency builds trust and agency; mystery protects signal quality and maintains engagement. The system optimizes for information gain — which means it must protect the conditions under which authentic behavior is observed.

The fundamental tension: if a student knows exactly what the system is measuring, they may perform for the measurement rather than authentically. This contaminates the Ledger. But if they know nothing, they cannot develop the meta-cognitive relationship with their own learning that is the long-term goal.

The resolution: disclose the structure, withhold the current state. The student should always know that the system selects sessions based on their trajectory. They should never know what the system currently thinks their trajectory is until they have enough sessions to discuss it meaningfully.

Part I · Section 4
Chemical Reactions — Students in Contact

A "chemical reaction" is what happens when two students with different cognitive profiles are placed in the same session. The Ledger currently treats sessions as individual measurements. But every multiplayer session is also a social experiment — the outcome is shaped by the specific combination of students, not just the config vector.

The system does not yet track this. But the architecture needs to account for it, because the wrong pairing contaminates both students' data, and the right pairing creates information gain that neither student could produce alone.

DEVELOPED + EMERGING
The high-Elo student may anchor or suppress the low-Elo student's authentic behavior. The low-Elo student's outcome is influenced by the high-Elo student's strategy, not their own cognition. Signal contamination for the lower student.
Ledger flag needed: session_composition = [HIGH, LOW] → outcome weight reduction for LOW student

QTY-dominant + SPT-dominant
Both students are competent overall but excel in different dimensions. The session forces each to operate in the other's stronger domain. High information gain for both — this is the ideal pairing for a mixed config session.

Part I · Section 6 — Questions for the designer
What I Need to Know Before Building Phase 1

These are the decisions that cannot be made from the architecture alone. They require your judgment about the students, the context, and the educational philosophy.

Q·A — Age & Cognitive Profile
Who are the students? What is the age range, and what do you know about their cognitive baseline?
The terminal language, zone names, and transparency dial all depend on who is reading them. A 12-year-old and a 17-year-old need different language. A student who has been told the system is measuring them needs different messaging than one who thinks it's just a game.
Q·B — What Do Students Currently Think the System Is?
Right now, before Phase 1 is implemented — what do students believe Constellation is? A game? An educational tool? Do they know sessions are being selected for them?
The terminal message for a student who already knows "this session was chosen for me" is completely different from a student encountering that idea for the first time. The first session to show a terminal message is the most important one to get right.
Q·C — The INVERSION Reveal
INVERSION (negative multipliers) creates inverted scoring. Currently it runs silently. When — if ever — should a student learn that INVERSION sessions exist?
Options: (1) Never reveal — protect the signal permanently. (2) Reveal post-session — "that session had inverted rules, your instincts were correct but misapplied." (3) Reveal to Alpha track only. The terminal currently says "something is different" without naming it. Is that enough?
Q·D — Social Dynamics in Your Specific Context
Are students competitive with each other? Do they play in fixed teams or rotating groups? Are there friendship dynamics that would affect pairing?
The chemical reaction architecture depends on whether you can control who plays with whom. If students self-select into teams, the social composition stage of curation is limited. If the teacher assigns sessions, full curation is possible.
Q·E — Session Frequency & Context
How often do students play? In what setting — classroom, after-school, at home? How long is a typical session?
The forgetting decay constant (τ=14d, ASSERTED) is the single biggest assumption the compiler makes. If students play twice a week, τ=14d may overestimate forgetting. If they play once a month, it may underestimate it.
Q·F — The Alpha Track Entry Criterion
Who should be on the Alpha track, and what should the entry process feel like to the student?
Alpha track students are ready to reason about the system itself. The application process and mystery calibration are designed. What triggers eligibility? A session count threshold? A teacher nomination? And critically: does the student apply, or are they invited?
Q·G — How Much Should the Terminal Explain Itself?
The terminal currently says things like "the system wants to know if you shifted with the conditions." Should the terminal ever explain what it means by "the system"? Or does the opacity serve the experience?
Spectrum: (1) Pure oracle — speaks and never explains. (2) Minimal context — "this is a cognitive development system" and nothing more. (3) Earned transparency — reveals more language over time as the student demonstrates they can handle it.
Q·H — What Happens When a Student Asks "Why Did I Get This Session?"
A curious student will ask the teacher. What should the teacher say?
Option A: "The system decides" (deflects, preserves mystery). Option B: "It's based on your history" (truthful but vague). Option C: "It thinks you're ready for something harder in [area]" (partially reveals the model). The answer determines how much the teacher is a translator between the student and the system.
PART II — SIGNAL INTEGRITY: TESTING, THE BOUNDARY & PROGRESSIVE DISCLOSURE

The Ledger is the boundary. Everything outside it can be explored freely. Everything inside it must be authentic. This section defines what testing is, who can do it, what it can observe, and what it must never touch — and how access to system knowledge expands progressively as students develop.

Part II · Section 1
Why Pre-Testing Destroys Signal

The system's One Principle is argmax E[KL(posterior ‖ prior)] — maximize expected information gain about the student's cognitive model per session. This principle assumes one thing absolutely: the behavior observed in a session reflects genuine real-time cognition.

Pre-testing violates this assumption at the root. A student who has already played a map against the bot before their assigned session is not discovering strategy — they are executing a remembered one. The cognitive task has changed from formation under uncertainty to retrieval under certainty. The Ledger entry produced looks identical to an authentic session but measures a completely different thing.

This is not a minor signal degradation. It is a category error. The posterior update the compiler computes from a pre-tested session is wrong — not noisy, but wrong. It updates the student's model in the direction of "good at QTY" when the correct update might be "good at remembering QTY solutions." These are different cognitive skills and they require different development paths.

The constraint is not about preventing cheating. It is about preserving the epistemic validity of the Ledger. A system that cannot distinguish authentic signal from rehearsed signal cannot serve its function — regardless of how sophisticated the compiler becomes.

Part II · Section 2
The Ledger Boundary — What Exists on Each Side

The Ledger boundary is the single architectural invariant of this system. It divides everything into two categories: observations that enter the student model, and exploration that does not. Crossing the boundary in the wrong direction is the only true integrity failure.

Implementation note: the sandbox mode is enforced by the sandbox: true flag on the session. Any session with this flag is excluded from all Ledger writes — the game runs identically but _writeSessionObservations is skipped entirely. This is not a soft filter applied at query time; it is a hard skip at write time. A sandbox session that accidentally writes is the only unrecoverable integrity failure in the system.

Part II · Section 3
The Four Principles of Testing Integrity
Principle 1
The Sandbox is not a lesser version of the real session. It is a different system entirely.
The sandbox and the real session share the same game engine, the same map files, and the same bot AI. But they are separated by the Ledger boundary. In the sandbox, you are studying the environment. In the real session, the environment is studying you. These are not gradations — they are categorically different modes of operation. Any UI that makes them look similar is a design failure.
Principle 2
Discovery during a session is signal. Discovery before a session is contamination.
A student who notices the bot is passive in round 1 and adjusts their strategy accordingly is demonstrating real-time cognitive flexibility — exactly what the system is designed to observe. That observation IS the signal. The same student who knew the bot was TURTLER before the session starts is demonstrating memory retrieval, not cognition. The observation looks identical in the Ledger but means something completely different. Temporal order relative to session start is the only distinction — and it is absolute.
Principle 3
The bot must be partially unpredictable at the student level — not as a design feature, but as a mathematical requirement.
If bot behavior were fully deterministic and knowable in advance, pre-testing would produce a complete map from (map, config) → (optimal strategy). Any student who had access to this map would play identically regardless of their actual cognitive capacity. The system would measure map-knowledge, not cognitive skill. Some degree of bot unpredictability is therefore not a limitation to be engineered away — it is a requirement to be preserved.
Principle 4
Retrospective access is never contaminating. Prospective access always is.
A student who studies their own past sessions to understand the bot's behavior pattern is reading signal that already exists in the Ledger. Their reading cannot change what was written. A student who uses that understanding to predict and pre-adapt to a future session is using past signal to corrupt future signal. The direction of time relative to the Ledger write is the invariant. Everything retrospective is safe. Everything prospective is contaminating.
Part II · Section 4
Progressive Unlock Tiers — How Access Expands Over Time

The constraint on student knowledge is not permanent. It is a scaffold that comes down as the student develops. The system uses four tiers, each unlocked by demonstrated development, not by time elapsed. The tiers are not communicated to students as a ladder — the unlock happens through what they see, not through an announcement that they've leveled up.

Each tier is justified directly by argmax E[KL]: the access granted at each tier is exactly the access that produces maximum information gain for the student's cognitive model at that stage, without contaminating the signal the Ledger needs to function.

Part II · Section 6
Bot Opacity — What Students Can Discover vs. Pre-Know

The bot is not fully opaque — it cannot be, because its behavior is observable during play. The design question is: what information about the bot should be available before play begins, vs. what must be discovered during play?

The answer follows directly from Principle 2: anything that changes first-move behavior is prospective and must be withheld. Anything that becomes visible through play is retrospective and acceptable to reveal after.

Bot aggression level WITHHELD pre-session
Aggression determines how early and aggressively the bot expands. Knowing it before round 1 allows the student to pre-select a counter-strategy (aggression=2 → expand freely early; aggression=8 → defend immediately). This eliminates the first-round diagnostic signal entirely — the system cannot observe the student's natural opening behavior.
changes opening moveeliminates calibration signal
Bot profile name WITHHELD always
Profile names (TURTLER, CONTESTER, ACCELERANT) are system-internal labels. Students should never see them — not because the information is harmful but because the labels create cognitive anchors. "You're facing a CONTESTER" shifts the student's attention to the label, not to the actual game state.
creates label-seeking behavioradmin-internal only
That a bot is present VISIBLE — always
Students should always know whether they are playing against a bot or a human. This is an ethical requirement, not a strategic one. Deceiving students about whether their opponent is human would violate the trust the system depends on. The terminal indicates "bot session" vs "multiplayer session" at session start.
ethical floordoes not affect cognitive signal
Bot behavior during play OBSERVABLE — always
Observing the bot's moves and adapting to them is exactly the cognitive work the system is designed to measure. A student who notices the bot is passive and shifts to aggressive expansion is showing strategic adaptation. This is the signal. Preventing in-session bot observation would prevent the student from playing strategically at all.

"In my last 5 RADICAL sessions, the bot started by contesting the northwest cluster every time" — this retrospective pattern recognition is itself a cognitive signal. Safe because it affects past-session interpretation, not future-session opening moves.

If this reached students, even retrospectively, it would allow collective pre-testing through shared knowledge — a student who learned from their peer what the bot does on a map they haven't played yet has effectively pre-tested it.

Part II · Section 7 — Prototype
The Admin Sandbox — Specification & Interface

The sandbox is the admin's tool for testing map-bot combinations before assigning them to students. It is architecturally identical to a real session — same game engine, same bot AI, same config vector — with one invariant: no Ledger writes, ever. The sandbox flag is set at session creation and cannot be cleared at runtime.

The sandbox exposes signals the real session also captures, but displays them in real time because here they serve design validation, not student assessment. The signals visible in the sandbox are the same signals written to the Ledger in real sessions — but their audience is the designer, not the compiler.

Part II · Section 8
Retrospective Access — What Alpha Students Can Study

The Alpha track exists because some students are ready to reason about the system. Retrospective access — reading their own completed session data — is the bridge between "player" and "analyst." It is always safe because the Ledger entries being read are already written. Nothing the student learns retrospectively can contaminate the data they are reading.

What it can contaminate is future sessions — if the student uses retrospective patterns to pre-load strategy for a map they haven't played yet. The constraint is therefore topological: retrospective access is safe for maps already played; it becomes prospective contamination when the student applies it to maps they haven't played.

The retrospective access gradient maps directly to the singularity. A student with full retrospective access who has played enough sessions to have stable Elos across all modes has everything they need to compile their own sessions. This is the Alpha track's endpoint. At that point, the student's retrospective analysis of bot behavior, zone patterns, and compiler decisions is not contamination — it is the student operating as their own compiler, which is the system's stated final goal.
