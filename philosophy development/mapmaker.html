Section 1 — Full mechanical model
What the AI Must Understand — Complete Game Mechanics

The mapmaker AI cannot produce strategically meaningful maps without a complete understanding of how the game works at the mechanical level. Every design decision — star placement, connection topology, HQ position — cascades into strategic affordances. This section is the mechanical ground truth the AI must operate from.

Stars (nodes)
Stars are capturable positions. A team claims a star by moving a piece onto it. Stars have a type: type 1 (capturable, no special function) and type 2 (HQ — headquarters, high strategic value). Stars do not move. Their positions define the map's strategic space.
Map design lever: star density, cluster shape, spatial distribution
Lines (edges)
Lines define which stars are adjacent — i.e., which moves are legal. A piece can move from star A to star B only if there is a line between them. The edge structure determines pathways, chokepoints, and the reachability of each star. Degree = number of edges at a node.
Map design lever: connectivity, degree distribution, bridge structures
Scoring multipliers
count_mult: points per captured star. dist_mult: points per unit of total distance between your stars. hq_mult: points for capturing or proximity to HQ stars. dest_mult: points for destroying opponent's stars. The config vector sets these — the map determines which multipliers are naturally rewarding.
Map design lever: HQ placement determines hq_mult viability; spread determines dist_mult viability
Moves & steals
Each team gets a fixed move pool per round (config: moves). A steal recaptures a star held by the opponent (costs extra from move pool, limited by steals). High steal limits favor contested maps; low steal limits favor spreading and holding.
Map design lever: chokepoint density affects how often steals are contested
Rounds & round length
Sessions have a fixed number of rounds (rounds, default 10). Each round has a time limit (round_length_s). Longer rounds favor deliberate planning; shorter rounds favor fast heuristics. Maps with complex topology penalize short-round play more.
Map design lever: high avg_degree maps are harder to read under time pressure
HQ placement
HQ stars (type 2) can be fixed (embedded in map JSON — both teams see the same HQ positions from game start) or dynamic (teams place their own HQ at the start of the game). Maps with 0 type-2 stars use dynamic HQ placement. Fixed HQ maps create deterministic fortress dynamics.
Map design lever: fixed HQs favor FRT mode; dynamic HQs add positional decision layer
Bot AI (current)
Bot AI is client-side, driven by aggression (0–10). High aggression = expand fast, contest early. Low aggression = consolidate. The bot has no deep strategy awareness — it responds to local state, not global map topology. This is Phase 1 reality; Phase 2 bot AI is strategy-profile-driven.
Map design implication: maps where early expansion dominates reward high-aggression bots more than maps where position matters
Wormholes
Wormhole pairs are long-range connections between non-adjacent stars. A piece that activates a wormhole teleports to its pair. Wormholes create non-local strategic options — the map's topology is no longer purely geographic. 6 Syn Wormholes.json is the only current map with wormhole edges.
Map design lever: wormhole pair distance and position define the teleportation advantage

The key insight for map design: the map determines which scoring strategies are viable, but the config vector determines which strategies are rewarded. A map with no HQ stars makes high hq_mult configurations irrelevant. A map with all stars clustered makes high dist_mult configurations low-reward. The mapmaker must design maps where the target mode's scoring strategy is genuinely viable.

Section 3 — Strategic patterns
Strategic Catalogue — What Each Map Should Do

Every map in the catalogue is a strategic intent, not just an aesthetic choice. When you ask the mapmaker for a map, you are asking it to create an environment where a specific cognitive challenge is the dominant experience. The map design follows from the strategic intent.

Strategic TypeTopology RequirementsPrimary ModeCognitive TargetAvoid
EXPANSION MAP High star count (80+), medium density, no large clusters, distributed stars with few chokepoints QTY Rapid territory coverage, move sequencing, opportunity cost under limited moves Large dead zones, disconnected regions, star clusters that reward camping
DISTANCE MAP Wide spread across canvas, stars placed near edges, long corridors, HQs at opposite corners SPT Spatial awareness, maximizing distance between held stars, resisting opponent's distance plays Central clustering, symmetric placement (makes spatial reasoning too simple)
FORTRESS MAP Fixed HQ stars (type 2), multiple HQ pairs, HQs proximate to each other, defensive corridors near HQs FRT Defensive positioning, HQ protection, contesting opponent HQ proximity HQs at edges with no natural defense routes, symmetric maps (fortress requires directional tension)
DESTRUCTION MAP High connectivity (avg_degree ≥ 5), many intersecting paths, high contention zones at center DST Aggressive recapture, denying opponent positions, scoring through opponent star removal Star deserts with no contested zones, low connectivity (limits steal pathways)
CORRIDOR MAP Long chains of stars with few branch points, defined bottleneck chokepoints, clear terrain lanes SPT / FRT Chokepoint identification, lane control, commitment decisions at forks Too many parallel corridors (dilutes the chokepoint decision), open areas where corridors connect
CLUSTER MAP Dense sub-graphs connected by sparse bridges, distinct regional territories, inter-cluster connections QTY / DST Regional control, deciding which cluster to contest, steal efficiency between clusters Single mega-cluster (no regional structure), fully uniform distribution (no cluster identity)

The mapmaker receives a directive — a structured specification of what the map should accomplish — and produces a valid map JSON through a multi-stage pipeline. Each stage is a distinct operation that can be validated before moving to the next.

HQ placement
If fixed HQs are required (FRT mode): place type-2 stars at positions that create intended fortress dynamics. For symmetric maps: mirror HQ positions around the canvas center. For asymmetric maps: place HQs at positions that create unique defensive challenges per side. Then upgrade those stars from type 1 → type 2 in the stars array.

Edge construction
Connect stars using a proximity graph (Delaunay triangulation or k-nearest neighbors), then prune/add edges to hit the target avg_degree and chokepoint structure. Ensure full connectivity: run BFS from any star — all stars must be reachable. Remove any isolated components by adding minimal bridging edges.

Topology validation
Run the topology analyzer (same algorithm as maps/generate-topology.js) on the generated map. Check: star_count in range, avg_degree ≥ 2.0, hq_count matches directive, all constraints satisfied. Compute mode_affinity and verify target mode affinity ≥ 0.65. Reject and regenerate if any check fails.
Step 5

Strategic simulation
Run a simple simulation: place a greedy-expansion agent on each team. Measure: which mode's scoring strategy scores highest? Are the intended chokepoints actually contested? Are there dominant strategies that make the game non-competitive? If the simulation reveals a broken map (one strategy wins by >70%), revise the layout.

Apply aesthetic constraints: organic vs. geometric layouts, named regions, visual identity. This does NOT change the strategic structure — it only adjusts star positions within their valid placement zones to produce a visually intentional result. The title and author are written to the JSON.

Section 5 — Image input
Image → Map — Converting Visual Input to Strategic Space

The most powerful mapmaker input is an image: a photograph, a hand-drawn sketch, a real-world map, a geometric shape. The AI converts visual structure into game topology using a consistent pipeline. This allows non-technical users to define spatial intent through drawing rather than coordinate specification.

What the image defines: the spatial distribution of territory, not the game mechanics. An image of a river delta defines: areas of density (where clusters will go), corridors (where connections will run), and open space (where stars will be sparse). The AI interprets the image as a spatial probability field, not a literal point-placement instruction.

Image ElementGame ElementTransformation rule
Dense region (dark area, complex detail)High star countPlace more stars in proportion to pixel density in that region. Density threshold: ASSERTED at 0.6 relative to image max. ASSERTED
Edge / boundary (detected via Canny or similar)Star placement along boundaryStars are placed along detected edges to preserve the shape's outline as strategic territory.
Line / corridor (thin connected region)Chain of stars with linear connectivityStars placed along corridor centerline, connected sequentially. Corridor width determines avg_degree at each star.
Intersection point (where corridors cross)High-degree node (potential chokepoint)Intersection receives connections from all corridors meeting there — creates natural strategic junction.
Isolated region (separate from main body)Cluster connected by bridge edgesIsolated regions become clusters. Minimum 1 bridge edge from each isolated cluster to main graph (connectivity requirement).
Symmetry axis (detected bilateral or rotational symmetry)Mirrored map topologyIf image is symmetric, the star/edge placement mirrors across the axis. HQs placed symmetrically on opposite sides.
Focal point (high contrast center, labeled landmark)High-value contested zoneHQ stars (type 2) or dense star cluster placed at focal points. Creates natural strategic center of gravity.

Image input directive format: the human provides an image alongside a strategic directive. The AI extracts spatial structure from the image, then applies the strategic directive to determine which extracted elements become which game structures. Example: "use this image of the London Tube map as the topology, make it a CORRIDOR map for FRT mode with fixed HQs at Paddington and Canary Wharf."

The AI must not attempt pixel-perfect recreation of the image. It must interpret spatial intent and convert it to a playable game graph. The result should be inspired by the image, not a literal trace of it.

Section 6 — Information balance
Information Balance in Map Design

A map communicates information to players. How much information a map reveals — and at what cost — is itself a design dimension. Maps exist on a spectrum from fully legible (every strategic decision is obvious) to discovery-based (optimal strategy requires exploration and inference).

The system's goal (argmax E[KL]) benefits from discovery-based maps: when the student must figure out the map's strategic structure, their process of discovery is itself informative. A map where the optimal move is always obvious produces low information gain — the student's behavior reveals nothing about their cognitive model.

Map Legibility LevelWhat students immediately seeWhat requires discoveryInformation gain
TRANSPARENT
e.g. small symmetric 20-star grid		 All strategic structure visible from board state. Optimal moves are obvious on inspection. Nothing — execution is the only challenge. Low — behavior mostly reveals execution speed, not strategy formation.
LEGIBLE
e.g. Castle Conundrum		 General structure apparent. Major clusters and connections visible. Optimal chokepoint sequence, resource priority ordering. Medium — reveals whether student recognizes structure; less about how they prioritize.
COMPLEX
e.g. CITYSCAPE		 Local neighborhood structure. Global structure not immediately apparent. Global map understanding, regional strategy, efficient path planning. High — reveals spatial reasoning, global-local integration.
DISCOVERY-BASED
e.g. LOTR ROSE		 Immediate neighbors only. Strategic structure must be built from experience. Map structure itself — the student must discover the topology as they play. Highest — every move is an information-gathering action about the map AND an attempt to score. The student's exploration strategy IS the signal.

Directive parameter for legibility: when requesting a map, the designer specifies legibility: "transparent" | "legible" | "complex" | "discovery". This controls:

  • Star density (higher density = more complex to survey)
  • Cluster size (larger clusters = more local complexity)
  • Degree distribution variance (uniform degree = legible; high variance = complex)
  • Canvas utilization (tightly clustered = legible; spread across canvas = discovery)
