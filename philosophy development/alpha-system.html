Zone Names in the Terminal — Making the System Feel Alive

The zone names (RADICAL, ECHO, PARALLEL, SHIFTING, REMATCH) should appear in the terminal as declarations, not labels. The system does not explain what they mean. It states them as fact. Students will notice them, compare them, and theorize about them. The vocabulary becomes the community's shared language through this process of reverse-engineering — not through being taught.

The Detective Framework — What the Analysis Must Cover

The detective framing is not decorative. It is the correct cognitive register for this task. A detective does not prescribe — they observe, infer, and explain. The analysis that says "they should have connected their HQs faster" is a command. The analysis that says "the HQ network never extended past the third cluster, which left distance score on the table in every round after round 4" is detective work. One tells. One shows.

Analysis Structure

PHASE 1 — Pre-Session (Planning Stage)

What did you observe about the team's approach before the session started? Did they appear to have a shared plan? What strategy does their opening seem to suggest?
Note: this phase may not always be visible. When it is, it is the most revealing — a team that does not discuss strategy before a FORTRESS session is a different kind of team than one that argues about HQ placement.

PHASE 2 — Round by Round (Playing Stage)

Walk through each round as a detective. What decisions were made? Where did the plan hold and where did it break down? What did individual players contribute?
You are not narrating the game. You are reading what the decisions reveal about the team's model of the session. A team that keeps expanding in a FORTRESS session may not have understood the multipliers.

PHASE 3 — Team Dynamics

How effectively did the team coordinate? Who drove decisions? Were there moments where individual play diverged from team strategy? What did this cost them?

PHASE 4 — Detective Observations

What did you notice that the players probably didn't notice themselves? These are your most valuable observations. Something that was consistent across rounds. A pattern in decision timing. A resource they consistently left unused.

PHASE 5 — Course Correction

If you were giving them one piece of direction after round 3 — not after the session, during it — what would you say? Why round 3 specifically? What did the data show by then?
Round 3 is significant: early enough to change the session, late enough to have real data. A good answer demonstrates both strategic understanding and pedagogical timing.

Evaluation — Surface vs Deep

Analyses are evaluated on a three-level scale. The evaluation is not shown to the applicant — only the outcome (accepted / not yet / feedback). This preserves the mystery of what the system values.

Surface Analysis Not Alpha
Names events without interpreting them. Prescribes corrections without explaining why they are corrections. Focuses on outcome (score) rather than process (decisions). Shows no evidence of understanding what the session was training.

Example: "They lost because they didn't claim enough stars and their opponents stole from them. They should have played more aggressively."
Mode-Aware Analysis Close
Demonstrates understanding of the session's scoring mechanic. Identifies decisions inconsistent with the mode. Suggests corrections that are mode-appropriate. Does not yet demonstrate cross-session or cross-mode awareness.

Example: "In a FORTRESS session the HQ distance matters most, and they spent too many moves on star count in the middle rounds. Better HQ timing from round 2 would have changed the network by round 6."
Strategic-Cognitive Analysis Alpha ◈
Demonstrates understanding of what cognitive skill the session was developing and how the team's decisions reveal their current model. Identifies behavioral patterns across rounds. Makes specific, round-referenced observations. The detective observations section contains something the players could not have told you themselves.

Example: "The team's HQ placement in round 2 was geometrically sound, but their expansion in rounds 3–5 was volume-oriented rather than connectivity-oriented — they were playing QTY logic inside an FRT session. The pivot came in round 6 when their network distance score fell behind, but by then the map topology made late connections expensive. Their opponent made fewer total moves but built a longer network. The team's model of what the session was rewarding didn't update until the score told them it was too late to act on it."
Why the Alpha analysis preserves the mystery. A student analyzing others never learns "here is what the system is measuring about you." They demonstrate that they already know what the game rewards at a strategic level. The deeper mystery — what the terminal is tracking in their own behavioral signals, what INVERSION inverts, what an ECHO means vs a RADICAL — remains opaque. The detective work operates at the game's surface level. The system's deep level requires the student's own experience to decode.

Alpha Application — The Form

CONSTELLATION — ALPHA APPLICATION
Submit a constructive analysis of a team's Constellation session. You will be reviewing both the planning stage and the playing stage. Your analysis is evaluated not on whether the team won or lost, but on the quality of your observations. Alpha status is awarded for demonstrated understanding, not for performance.

Session Reference

Which session are you analyzing? (Session ID, date, or description)

Phase 1 — Before the Session

If you observed the planning stage: What was the team's apparent strategy going in? Did they have a shared understanding of what the session required? What did their preparation reveal?

Phase 2 — The Playing Stage

Walk through the session as a detective. What did the team do in each round? Where did decisions align with the session's requirements, and where did they not? Be specific — name rounds, name players if possible, name the decision and why you read it as you do.

Phase 3 — Team Coordination

How effectively did the team work together? Who drove the key decisions? Were there moments where individual play diverged from team strategy? What was the effect?

Phase 4 — Your Detective Observations

What did you notice that the team probably didn't notice themselves? This is the most important part of your application. A pattern across rounds. A resource consistently left unused. A decision that made sense locally but cost them at the session level. Something specific and verifiable — not general advice.

Phase 5 — Course Correction

If you could pause the session at the end of round 3 and say one thing to the team — not after, during — what would it be? Why round 3? What did you know by then that they apparently didn't?

Anything Else

Anything your analysis didn't capture. Questions you had. Things you noticed but couldn't fully explain.
Your application will be reviewed by the class administrator and, if other Alphas exist, by them as well. You will receive a response — not a score, but a reaction. Applications are not ranked; they are either accepted, returned with specific feedback, or declined with direction toward what to develop further. There is no penalty for applying and being declined. The application itself is a development exercise.

Mystery Calibration — What the System Reveals and When

The mystery has two layers. The first layer is strategic: what does each mode reward? This is discoverable by playing. A student who doesn't understand that FORTRESS rewards HQ distance will see it in their score breakdown. This layer is meant to be solved. The second layer is systemic: what is the terminal measuring about how you play, why do you get a RADICAL vs an ECHO, what does INVERSION invert exactly? This layer is meant to be decoded slowly, through accumulation, comparison, and community. The Alpha system operates at the first layer. The second layer remains opaque even to Alphas.

Layer 1 — Visible to all

  • Score breakdown by multiplier component
  • Round-by-round scores
  • Win/loss and rank
  • Mode name (FORTRESS, SPATIAL, etc.)
  • Zone names in terminal (ECHO, RADICAL)
  • Bot archetypes and aggression
The game. What happened. Students should be able to understand why they won or lost from this information alone.

Layer 2 — Decoded through play

  • Zone name meanings (discovered by comparing messages across sessions)
  • What produces a RADICAL vs ECHO (detectable from outcome patterns)
  • Mode adjacency (students who play all 6 notice SHIFTING patterns)
  • Codename vocabulary by mode (learnable from accumulation)
  • Overlay indicators (students learn INVERSION markers)
The meta-game. What the system is doing. Decoded through sustained attention and community comparison.

Layer 3 — Never fully revealed

  • Exact signal thresholds (when does RADICAL trigger?)
  • The specific behavioral signals being tracked per session
  • The student's exact Elo and how it's computed
  • Why PARALLEL rather than SHIFTING was selected
  • The overlay probability formula
  • The forgetting decay rate
The system's internals. Admin-only. The opaque layer that keeps the second game permanently interesting even for advanced students.

The Zone Name Calibration Table

ZoneWhat terminal saysWhat student can inferWhat student cannot infer
REMATCH "The system requires one more signal. REMATCH." Something was unresolved. Similar session coming. Exact threshold that triggered REMATCH vs ECHO. Whether difficulty changed.
ECHO "ECHO. The system is returning you to unfinished territory." Similar but not identical to last session. Close outcome triggered this. Whether ECHO means same template or same mode. Difficulty adjustment.
PARALLEL "PARALLEL. Same destination. New route." Same mode, probably different template. Solid performance triggered this. Exact template selection logic. Why PARALLEL rather than SHIFTING.
SHIFTING "SHIFTING. Adjacent territory. Prior knowledge is partially applicable." Mode change coming, but related to last mode. Comfortable win triggered this. What "adjacent" means exactly. The mode adjacency map.
RADICAL "RADICAL departure. What you learned here does not apply there." Complete mode change. Either dominated or hit a streak limit. Whether it was triggered by performance or streak. Which mode is coming.
The calibration principle. Each zone name tells the student something true about what is happening, without telling them why the system decided this or how to produce a different outcome next time. The student who understands what RADICAL means strategically — "I performed too well, the system is moving me somewhere harder" — has decoded Layer 2. The student who understands exactly what threshold triggers RADICAL has found Layer 3. Layer 3 is never fully decodable from the terminal alone. This ensures the second game never ends.
