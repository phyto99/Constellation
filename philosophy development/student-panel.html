The Student Panel

Foundation
Why a student panel exists at all

The admin panel exists for teachers and researchers: people who configure sessions on behalf of students, observe from outside, and have responsibilities the student doesn't have. The student panel exists for a completely different reason: because the act of choosing a session is itself developmental.

A student who picks "play with friends tonight" is making a social and cognitive choice. A student who adjusts rounds or picks a map they haven't played is exercising preference and judgment. These choices are data — not Ledger data in the technical sense, but real information about what the student values, what they're curious about, and how they relate to the system.

If we take away those choices and let only the admin configure sessions, we lose something real. We also lose engagement: a student who built their own session has more investment in it than one who was dropped into one.

The student panel is not a restricted admin panel. It is a different instrument entirely — designed for a different actor, with a different relationship to the system. Its job is to make good sessions accessible to students while protecting the measurements the Ledger depends on.

Boundary
What the student panel is not

Not the admin panel with fields grayed out. That approach fails on every dimension: it feels condescending, it makes the restrictions visible and therefore gameable ("why is this grayed out? what does it do?"), and it frames the student as a lesser admin rather than as a different kind of user entirely. The student panel is built from its own first principles.

Not a dashboard of the student's cognitive model. That's a separate question — what students can see about their own data — and it's answered in the progressive unlock tier system. The panel is about creating sessions, not about viewing data. These two concerns should not share a screen.

Not a simplified toy version of the game. The sessions students create from this panel are identical in technical terms to sessions the admin creates. Same engine, same bots, same Ledger writes. The difference is in what choices the student makes and how the system frames those choices.

The student panel and the admin panel are parallel instruments for different actors, not a hierarchy where students get fewer controls. Students get different controls — the ones appropriate to their role in the system.
Core Design
The two acts a student performs

Every session a student creates from this panel is one of two things: personal training or hosting for others. These are genuinely different acts with different optimization targets. They should be treated as distinct, not unified under a single settings form.

This is philosophically coherent (the compiler can optimize for one player even when others are present) but raises questions about fairness: is it fair that one player's development is being served while others are in the room? The System's Voice
The compiler recommendation and what it should communicate

The terminal is the system's voice. In the student panel it plays exactly the same role as the pre-session terminal in the game itself: it tells the student what kind of experience they're about to have, in the system's aesthetic, without revealing internal variables.

But in the panel, it has a second function: it is the compiler's recommendation, made visible as a session the student can accept or build on. The student doesn't have to use it. They can ignore it entirely and configure something different. But the recommendation is always there, and always offered first.

The recommendation is honest about its own limitations. The system does not claim omniscience. If the student has played fewer than 5 sessions, the terminal says so — "the system does not know you well enough yet to make a confident recommendation." The recommendation becomes more specific as the Ledger grows.

──────────────────────────────────────── CONSTELLATION cognitive development system session 024 zone RADICAL signal new territory · gap detected The system has been watching a gap form. One dimension of your thinking has outpaced another. This session addresses it directly. This will feel different. That is the point. ──────────────────────────────────────── 23 sessions recorded · recommendation based on your data

The terminal does not say "the compiler selected QTY mode with dest_mult=−200." It says what the student will experience. The internal variables are the system's concern, not the student's. This is not obscurity — it is the transparent medium principle. The student is the instrument. The student experiences the session. They don't need to read the instrument's calibration settings.

What the panel offers after the terminal: a one-click "launch this recommendation" button, and a separate section where they can build something different. The recommendation is never hidden, never the only option, and never imposed.

Open: If a student consistently deviates from the compiler's recommendation — always choosing familiar maps, always picking Easy bots — should the system note this as a signal? It is arguably informative: a student who avoids RADICAL sessions even when the compiler recommends them is demonstrating something real about their relationship to challenge. Not in the current implementation. Worth flagging for Era 12.
The Hard Problem
Multiplayer: whose development does the system serve?

All games are multiplayer. This is not an edge case — it is the default. The question of whose cognitive model the compiler is optimizing for is therefore central to every session, not peripheral.

There is no clean answer. Here is the honest taxonomy:

Case 1
Creator only — no other Ledger data available
The compiler has data on the creator. The other players are new or anonymous. The correct approach: optimize for the creator's development, but set an engagement floor tuned for newcomers. The engagement floor is a hard constraint — below it, the session produces nothing for anyone. Above it, the compiler can optimize. Decided.
Case 2
All players known — ZPD intersection
The compiler has Ledger data for everyone in the room. The theoretically correct approach: find the config vector that maximizes the expected information gain summed across all players. In practice this requires knowing who's in the room before the session starts, which the two-phase flow (rough config → room → players join → live adjust → start) enables. The compiler re-runs as players join and updates the recommendation. Architecturally planned, not yet implemented.
Case 3
New players in the room — engagement-first
If any player in the room has fewer than 5 sessions, the system shifts to engagement-first configuration. More teams, kinetic scoring, accessible rules, no INVERSION, no Ithkuil. The reasoning: a new player's first session is their highest-information observation — the prior uncertainty is maximum. Getting them to play naturally and enjoy it matters more than optimizing the configuration for the experienced players in the room. The experienced players' Elo impact from one non-optimal session is negligible. The new player's first impression is not. Decided.
Case 4
Competitive context — target the highest player
When all players are experienced and the intent is competitive, the compiler targets the highest-Elo player's ZPD. Everyone else is stretched upward. The reasoning from the argmax E[KL] principle: the highest-Elo player has the most stable model — a session within their ZPD produces the most information about everyone's position relative to a known reference. This is philosophically consistent but potentially produces frustrating sessions for lower-Elo players who are over-challenged. Decided as default; may need a per-session override.
Deep tension: The compiler is fundamentally a single-student instrument. It optimizes argmax E[KL] for one posterior at a time. Extending it to multi-player optimization requires either (a) a summed objective across players — which is computationally tractable but philosophically questionable (whose development matters more?) — or (b) a separate multi-player compiler that treats the room as its own entity. Option (b) is the philosophically cleaner answer but significantly more complex. This is the correct framing of the problem: it's not "whose ZPD do we target" — it's "does a room of students have a joint cognitive state that the system can optimize for?" We don't know. This is an open research question, not a configuration decision.
What's Hidden
What the panel obscures, and whether each is justified

Every obscured element in the student panel is a claim: "revealing this would change what the measurement measures, or would produce sessions that harm the system's goals." These claims need to be evaluated individually, using the same test from the opacity model.

(HAL, CAESAR, etc.)		 Knowing the bot's named archetype creates a cognitive anchor — student plays against the label, not the game state. Partially justified. The label itself is probably low-risk. Core Tension
Agency is development. Signal is development. They conflict.

The student panel exists at a permanent tension: student agency is itself developmental (choosing, experimenting, deciding), but some student choices corrupt the signal the system needs to serve their development. You cannot fully resolve this tension. You can only manage where you draw the line and be explicit about why.

The line the current design draws: choices that affect the pre-session information state are constrained; choices about what kind of experience to have are free.

What this means in practice:

  • Student can choose any map from the list — but doesn't see the topology before selecting.
  • Student can add a bot and choose its difficulty — but doesn't know the exact bot type assigned.
  • Student can set rounds, round length, steals, teams, HQ — these don't corrupt cognitive signal.
  • Student cannot set exact multiplier values in Friends mode — but can see and influence the range.
  • Student cannot toggle INVERSION in Friends mode — but can in Solo Training (where they've consented to its use against themselves).

The deepest question this design has not fully answered: is restricting student choice paternalistic, or is it protective of a mechanism the student themselves would endorse if they understood it? The opacity model argues for the latter. A student who understands why INVERSION requires silence in a social session would probably agree with the restriction. But that understanding comes after the session, not before. The design asks for trust before it can offer the explanation.

The collaborative staircase — a session type where every player is working toward their own ceiling simultaneously, with no competitive rank — is not addressed anywhere in the student panel design. It is a first-class session mode philosophically (per the Synthesis document) but has no representation in the panel's controls. This is a gap.
Unresolved
Open questions before this panel is philosophically complete
Q1 — The deviation signal
Should the system record when a student overrides the compiler's recommendation?
A student who consistently deviates from RADICAL recommendations, always choosing ECHO instead, is showing something real: discomfort with the stretch zone, or metacognitive preference, or simply not trusting the system. Is this Ledger-worthy? It's not a cognitive performance signal — it's a metacognitive behavior signal. The evidence engine could record it as a SELF channel observation. Whether it should influence future compiler decisions is a separate question. OPEN
Q2 — Collaborative staircase
How does the student panel handle a session where everyone is working toward their own ceiling?
The two-mode design (Solo Training / Hosting for Friends) doesn't have a slot for the collaborative staircase. It is neither personal training (which targets one student's ZPD) nor social hosting (which targets the room's engagement). It requires per-player compiler outputs running simultaneously. No session type in the current panel enables this. Should there be a third mode? OPEN
Q3 — The joint compiler
Is the correct multi-player model a summed objective, a joint state, or something else entirely?
The current design answers this with heuristics (engagement floor, new-player detection, ZPD intersection). These are pragmatic but philosophically incomplete. The theoretically correct answer requires understanding whether a room of students has a jointly describable cognitive state — which we don't know yet. This question should be revisited after multi-game data accumulates and the multi-game architecture (Era 6) provides cross-player signal. OPEN — Era 12 or later
Q4 — Bot name visibility
Does showing the bot's character name (not its strategy description) corrupt the signal?
The current design hides the bot name entirely, substituting Easy/Standard/Hard. But "HAL" without its description ("steady, logical, dispassionate") may be low-risk — students who don't know the strategy can't exploit the label. Students who do know (because they've played many sessions) have earned that knowledge. This is analogous to zone vocabulary: the name itself is not the problem; the strategy description attached to it is. Consider showing bot name post-session. OPEN
Q5 — Multiplier transparency
Is hiding exact multipliers in Friends mode actually doing useful work?
The scoring weights are visible during the game anyway (or derivable from it). Pre-session knowledge of exact values may not provide meaningful additional advantage. The ±10% variation is the real protection — it prevents cross-session optimization even if students see the values. Consider showing the weights but keeping the variation. OPEN
Q6 — The panel's own signal
Should the configuration choices students make in this panel be Ledger-recorded as behavioral observations?
A student who always picks the longest possible sessions, always turns steals off, always picks "Surprise Me" for maps — these patterns are real. They belong in the SELF channel of the evidence engine. Currently nothing from the panel writes to the Ledger except the session itself. The panel interaction is a pre-session behavioral record that is currently being discarded. OPEN — high value, low implementation cost
